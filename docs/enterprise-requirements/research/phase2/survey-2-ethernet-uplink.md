## Survey 2: Standard Ethernet uplink — recommendation

**PHY interface/part:** prefer **SGMII** from the PolarFire SoC's hard MAC (GEM) over RGMII where practical. Each PolarFire SoC GEM reaches an external PHY two ways: (1) a **direct MSS SGMII** path through the dedicated MSS SERDES — no FPGA fabric/general-routing cost, but it cannot carry SyncE; or (2) **GMII/RGMII through fabric I/O lanes** (PolarFire's I/O lanes have built‑in RGMII-timing-compensation logic, so it's not raw-LUT-expensive, but it is still fabric/HSIO budget) [Microchip UG0687; PolarFire SoC Product Overview]. Given Appendix B.3/B.5's intent to keep fabric free for the TSN-switch/hardware-timestamping data plane, the MSS-SGMII path is the architecturally cleaner fit, and it is also the path Microchip's own **PolarFire SoC Icicle Kit reference design** uses (MSS SGMII → Microchip **VSC8662**, a dual‑port SGMII/1000BASE‑X‑to‑copper PHY, → RJ‑45) [Icicle Kit User Guide; ICICLE Kit Schematics; github.com/polarfire-soc/icicle-kit-reference-design]. Recommend **VSC8662** as the primary candidate on that basis (vendor‑proven pairing, schematic precedent, and the unused second port is free headroom for a future 2nd uplink/failover per REQ‑HUB‑COMMON‑050). If VSC8541/VSC8662‑family lifecycle risk (see Risks) is unacceptable, fall back to **TI DP83869HM** (SGMII+RGMII+copper/fibre bridge, 8kV IEC 61000‑4‑2 “high immunity” rating built into the PHY) at a real cost premium, or to plain **RGMII** with **KSZ9131RNXI** (cheapest well‑supported industrial‑temp option) if simplicity beats fabric conservation.

**Magnetics form:** **integrated shielded MagJack (with LEDs)**, not discrete transformer + separate jack. At this port count (one uplink, maybe a spare on ENT‑NET) the assembly‑simplicity and fewer‑solder‑joints argument for an integrated module outweighs the modest per‑unit BOM saving discrete magnetics offer only at real volume [l-p.com integrated‑vs‑separate guide; sunuprisecom.com MagJack‑vs‑discrete guide]. Spec the magnetics to ≥2× the IEEE 802.3 isolation floor (≥1500 Vrms; parts like Pulse HX1188FNL are rated to 6 kV) for margin [l-p.com isolation‑voltage explainer; Mouser HX1188FNL listing].

**Protection network:** magnetics isolation + standard Bob‑Smith termination (blocking caps) as the free primary defense, plus a low‑capacitance TVS array on the PHY side of the magnetics for ESD/IEC 61000‑4‑2, plus an optional 3‑electrode GDT at the jack for the cable‑shield‑to‑earth path — sized to office/rack‑adjacent levels, not building‑entrance/telecom‑grade. Full topology in the OQ‑14 section below.

**SFP verdict: do not populate by default in v1.** Document it as a studied‑and‑deferred option (satisfies REQ‑HUB‑NET‑030's "SFP option study"), not a locked exclusion. Reasoning in the BOM/verdict sections below.

### OQ‑14 enterprise closure proposal — specific protection topology

Proposed answer for the Phase‑4 spec edit (closes the Enterprise/MC half of OQ‑14, landing on **this** port per REQ‑HUB‑NET‑030, distinct from the consumer module‑RJ45 answer already locked in spec §2.4):

1. **Galvanic isolation is the primary, free defense.** IEEE 802.3 mandates ≥1500 Vrms (or equivalent 2250 Vdc/60s, or 10×2400V impulses) isolation in the magnetics between cable and board [IEEE 802.3af regulatory doc, ieee802.org; l‑p.com isolation explainer]. Specify magnetics/MagJack rated at least 2× that floor (e.g., HX1188FNL‑class parts are rated to 6 kV [Mouser HX1188FNL]). This isolation, combined with the standard Bob‑Smith center‑tap termination's blocking capacitors, means the port is **DC‑isolated from any injected voltage on the line pins by construction** — no dedicated "PoE protection" component is required.
2. **This is also the answer to "should the port survive accidental PoE injection": yes, by design, at ~$0 incremental BOM.** A standards‑compliant PSE will not apply power without detecting our (absent) PD signature resistor, so a compliant switch/injector never energizes the pins. The residual case is a non‑compliant **passive** injector that drives 24–57 V regardless of what's on the other end — multiple independent sources confirm the risk in that scenario is specifically for devices *without* magnetics/isolation, and that a normal Ethernet magnetics + termination front end is the standard mitigation [doingbetterbusiness.com PoE‑risk writeup; community.ui.com PoE‑injector‑damage thread]. Verify only that the Bob‑Smith blocking‑cap voltage rating clears 57 V with margin (standard practice uses ≥200 V‑rated parts).
3. **Add a low‑capacitance TVS array on the PHY side of the magnetics**, across all four differential pairs, for ESD and cable‑discharge events. Per‑line capacitance should stay in the low single‑digit pF range to avoid degrading gigabit signal integrity [digikey.com TVS‑diode‑for‑GbE article; blog.semtech.com Ethernet Protection Methodology — explicitly recommends placing TVS **after**, not before, the magnetics, since line‑side placement can convert common‑mode transients into differential‑mode ones]. Target IEC 61000‑4‑2 in the range compliant equipment ports commonly certify to (±8 kV contact / ±15 kV air) [microtype.io IEC 61000‑4‑5 surge‑circuits writeup]. Concrete part class: Semtech RClamp0524P/RClamp3361P or Bourns CDSOD323‑class GbE‑rated arrays, ~$0.30–0.80 each.
4. **Add a 3‑electrode GDT at the RJ‑45 shield‑to‑chassis‑earth path (line side)** sized to IEC 61000‑4‑5 Level 2 (~24 A, 8/20 µs) [digikey.com TVS article citing compliant‑device levels]. This is deliberately **not** building‑entrance/telecom‑grade (that class runs 10 kA‑class two‑stage GDT+TVS coordinated protection, sold as dedicated in‑line SPD accessories [prosurge.com; trilpeak.com GDT guide]) — the Hub's named target fleet is rack‑adjacent, structured‑cabling‑within‑one‑facility gear [customer-integration-audit-2026-07-01.md §1 finding 8], not a building‑entrance device, mirroring the same "deliberate misuse / already covered by infrastructure" reasoning the spec used to close the consumer half of OQ‑14 (spec §2.4). If a future customer segment needs true outdoor‑plant or inter‑building runs, push that to a documented external in‑line Ethernet SPD accessory (COTS parts like Citel/Prosurge/Tupavco RJ45 protectors exist for exactly this) rather than a board respin.
5. **This is a proposal, not a re‑derivation of a locked decision** — flag for owner ratification at the Phase‑4 spec edit alongside the PHY/magnetics choice, the same way the consumer OQ‑14 half was owner‑ratified.

### Confusion‑proofing note

The enterprise Hub now carries **two functionally unrelated RJ‑45 8P8C connector families on one board**: the LOCKED module‑to‑Hub star (CAN_H/CAN_L on pins 3/6, DETECT on pin 8, 5VSB on pin 1 — not Ethernet signaling at all, spec §2.1–§2.3) and the new northbound **standard IEEE 802.3 1000BASE‑T uplink** using true T568B Ethernet pinout. These must not be treated as interchangeable, and they are not electrically compatible even though the shell is identical:
- **Do not** let a technician assume the uplink can be patched into an available module port or vice versa. Plugging a real switch cable into a module port will simply fail to link (no valid 802.3 PHY partner on our CAN‑driven pins) — annoying but not obviously unsafe. The riskier direction is plugging a *module* (CAN transceiver actively driving pins 3/6, 5VSB present on pin 1) into a real switch port; most switch ports have their own isolation/ESD protection and will likely just fault or ignore it, but this is not a designed‑for interface and shouldn't be relied on to always be benign.
- **Recommend:** keep module ports as the existing default (locking‑boot, unkeyed, matches every other Hub tier — do not touch the locked pin table). Make the **single new uplink port** the visually distinct one: (1) a different jack bezel/boot color (e.g., the common industrial "blue = uplink/WAN" convention) sourced as a color variant of the same locking‑boot family to keep BOM/assembly impact near zero; (2) explicit board‑ and faceplate‑silkscreen text ("UPLINK — IEEE 802.3 1000BASE‑T", distinct from numbered module ports "1/2/3/4…"); (3) physical separation/grouping on the PCB and enclosure — uplink on a different edge/zone from the module bank, following the same convention most switches/routers already use to separate "uplink" from "downstream" ports. A custom mechanical key is **not** recommended for the uplink itself, since it must accept the customer's own standard structured‑cabling patch cords.
- Separately, note there is a **second, pre‑existing 100BASE‑T1 in the platform** that is unrelated to this survey: the module DETECT code table already reserves a "CAN+100BASE‑T1" encoding (10 kΩ) for the Max module tier riding single‑pair Ethernet over RJ‑45 pair 2 on the *module* star (OQ‑20/§6.11), which the audit explicitly firewalls from this uplink reversal [customer-integration-audit-2026-07-01.md §4.1]. If REQ‑HUB‑NET‑031's factory‑option 1000BASE‑T1 uplink is ever built, it needs its **own** distinct PHY (single‑pair automotive/industrial SPE, e.g. TI DP83TC81x‑class or Marvell 88Q211x‑class — out of this survey's scope) and should not be assumed to share silicon, connector, or labeling conventions with either the module‑side 100BASE‑T1 or the new standard uplink.

### Risks

- **VSC8541/VSC8662 family lifecycle.** One SKU checked (VSC8541XMV‑01) is already listed obsolete with a substitute part; this is Vitesse‑heritage silicon that has changed corporate hands twice (Vitesse → Microsemi → Microchip). Confirm current lifecycle/NRND status with a Microchip FAE before committing to VSC8662 as primary. TI's DP83867/DP83869 and Microchip's newer KSZ913x line show no such flag in this pass.
- **Realtek provenance/posture mismatch.** RTL8211FI‑CG is the cheapest RGMII option by a wide margin, but this tier's whole differentiation is PUF/DPA‑resistant secure boot, an Athena crypto coprocessor, CNSA‑compliant algorithms, and an NCSC‑reviewed design‑separation flow (spec App B.3). Putting a low‑cost Chinese‑fabless PHY directly on the uplink's differential pairs is a minor but real inconsistency with that posture; keep it only as a fallback for a possible future non‑security‑differentiated variant, not the Enterprise/MC default.
- **SGMII is a first SERDES‑class interface for this team's board flow.** Everything shipped so far in this repo is single‑ended or modest‑rate differential (USB FS/HS, RS‑485, CAN). SGMII at ~1.25 Gbps line rate wants controlled impedance, tighter length matching, and possibly AC‑coupling caps — not a blocker, but budget a verification/SI pass the existing boards haven't needed.
- **Direct‑MSS‑SGMII drops SyncE.** Confirmed directly from Microchip documentation. Likely irrelevant to CEC's telemetry use case today, but Appendix B.3's TSN/hardware‑timestamping ambition means this is a design fork to watch — if a future requirement wants SyncE specifically (distinct from PTP‑over‑MAC, which still works), the fabric‑transceiver SGMII path is needed instead, at a real fabric‑budget cost.
- **Pricing confidence is uneven.** Several numbers in the BOM table are extrapolated from adjacent quantity tiers or single‑unit distributor listings rather than a genuine 100‑piece quote (marked [unverified] throughout); several distributor pages returned no machine‑readable pricing at all in this pass (JS‑rendered tables). Re‑quote through a formal distributor BOM tool before the D‑ENT‑3 baseline is finalized.
- **Protection topology is sized to an assumption, not a spec fact.** The Level‑2/office‑grade IEC 61000‑4‑5 target assumes the named target fleet (rack‑adjacent, single‑building structured cabling). If sales ever needs to claim protection for a facility with real building‑entrance/outdoor‑plant exposure, this under‑protects and needs escalation to the heavier two‑stage topology or an external accessory SPD.
- **SFP verdict is reversible and owner‑gated.** REQ‑HUB‑NET‑030 explicitly still wants the "SFP option study" on record; this survey recommends against v1 population on target‑market grounds (Opengear/Lantronix‑class SFP‑bearing devices are aggregation‑tier console servers, a different network position than CEC's per‑machine Hub), not because SFP is technically difficult — PolarFire SoC's SGMII output is in fact a very natural fit for an SFP cage. Treat this as a documented, revisitable D‑gate rather than a closed door.

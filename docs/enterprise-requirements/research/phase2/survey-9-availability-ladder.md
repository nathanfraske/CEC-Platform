## Survey 9: MC availability ladder — recommendation per rung

**Scope honesty up front, because it governs every choice below.** The Hub's job here is PC power-telemetry aggregation, CAN module-bus service, RS-485 stream reception, northbound reporting, and tamper-log custody — not motion control, not a safety-instrumented function in the IEC 61508/ISO 26262 sense. "Fail-functional" for MC-Max means *monitoring continuity and log integrity survive one compute-node fault*, not that a hazardous physical process is being kept safe. The vocabulary below (2oo2, 1oo2D, TMR, voting) is borrowed from avionics/rail/PLC engineering because that is the best-documented body of practice for "keep computing correctly through one node fault," **not** because this product needs or will pursue a SIL/ASIL certification. I'd recommend the requirement register not cite IEC 61508/ISO 26262 levels for this ladder (unlike REQ-HUB-COMMON-096's IEC 62443 target, which *is* an appropriate framework match) — using safety-market part numbers is fine; implying a safety-market certification claim is not.

**Bottom line.** Recommend the **small safety-oriented MCU class** for the Rung-1 watchdog (not a bare supervisor IC — the requirement needs it to watch CAN health telemetry and write a tamper log, not just pulse a reset), sized to the **non-lockstep member of a family that scales into lockstep** rather than jumping straight to a certified lockstep part — the Appendix B.3 Hercules leaning is technically sound but not uniquely compelling once toolchain fit is weighed in. For Rung 2, REQ-HUB-COMMON-104's own wording ("a voting pair... arbitration involving the independent watchdog") has already implicitly picked **2oo2 duplex + arbiter over true 2oo3 TMR** — this survey validates that as the right economic and architectural choice and fills in the mechanism: checkpointed (not lockstep) state sync over PolarFire's already-free PCIe/CAN resources, a narrow voted-output boundary (tamper log + Appendix-D actuation only), and reconnect-tolerated northbound sessions.

## 1. Rung 1 (MC SKU): the independent compute watchdog

### 1.1 What "independent" must mean (the selection filter)

Three properties, all sourced from how the safety-electronics literature actually defines it, not just the spec's own words:

- **Own clock source.** IEC 61508-2's diagnostic-technique guidance for watchdogs specifically wants "a separate time base" — the point is that a clock failure on the *monitored* device must not silently disable the *monitor*. ST's own STM32 safety manuals make the distinction concrete at the register level: "the watchdog timer's clock should be independent of the main system clock, so a clock failure doesn't silently disable the safety mechanism. The IWDG's LSI oscillator qualifies as an independent clock source, while the WWDG's APB1-derived clock doesn't" [Analog Devices / ST safety-package sources]. That is the correct bar for REQ-103's "own clock": a genuinely separate oscillator (its own crystal or RC), not merely a separate clock *divider* off the SoC's tree.
- **Own supervised rail, not a fourth power source.** This does *not* mean adding a new physical power input beyond survey 4's three-source priority-OR — that would duplicate work already closed under OQ-53..56. It means the watchdog's own small regulator sits **downstream of the already-arbitrated +5VSB rail** (the rail REQ-HUB-COMMON-026's STANDBY posture already guarantees stays alive), electrically separate from whatever local regulator feeds the PolarFire core/DDR/SERDES rails, so a PolarFire-side brownout, sequencing fault, or regulator failure doesn't take the watchdog down with it. It should itself be monitored (PG-style) the same way survey 4's eFuse fronts monitor the three §2.9 sources.
- **Isolated I/O to force-safe the main SoC.** A GPIO/open-drain line into the SoC's own reset/enable pin is the minimum; for a hard force-to-STANDBY (not just a reset), the same lever survey 4 already built is reusable: the watchdog asserts the `EN` line on the MAIN_5V TPS25940-class eFuse front-end (survey 4 built exactly this pin for firmware's own commanded self-test) to pull the Hub off MAIN_5V and down onto the 5VSB-guaranteed STANDBY floor survey 1 defined. Recommend a **two-tier escalation**, mirroring the "error accumulator... react gracefully to minor issues while still triggering a hard reset when conditions warrant" pattern that's standard practice for challenge-response watchdogs [DigiKey/Analog Devices watchdog design guidance]: soft reset of the main SoC first (cheap, reversible, covers a hang), escalate to the hard eFuse-EN force-STANDBY only after N consecutive missed challenges (covers a SoC that won't even come back from reset).

### 1.3 Validating the Appendix B.3 Hercules leaning

The spec text itself is worth being precise about: Appendix B.3's exact sentence is *"This is consistent with Mission Critical already using a bare-metal Hercules safety coprocessor, so the top of the stack is already non-Linux."* I checked — **this is the only mention of "Hercules" anywhere in the Ground-Truth spec.** The §1 tier table's own Mission Critical row says only "ESP32-P4 plus crypto" / "Redundant power, CAN, trust"; nothing elsewhere specifies a Hercules part. So B.3's claim is better read as *"Hercules is a plausible example of the class,"* not as an already-locked decision — there's nothing upstream of it to be consistent *with*. Read that way, it holds up fine as an example, and my finding doesn't overturn it so much as confirm it needs the same owner-gate treatment PolarFire itself got (D-ENT-2): a named part-class recommendation for the owner to ratify, not a silent inheritance.

On the substance: Hercules **is** a legitimate, currently-Active, TÜV-certified choice, and its target-application language (railway signaling, PLCs) is exactly the vocabulary REQ-104 uses. But two things argue for treating it as one strong option among several rather than the default: (1) it is an aging core generation (Cortex-R4F/R5F silicon dating to the 2009–2013 timeframe) with no confirmed successor announced, and its toolchain is HALCoGen + Code Composer Studio (or FreeRTOS) — a **separate ecosystem from Zephyr**, which survey 6 already picked for the Hub's own RTOS; **Zephyr's TI-Hercules support is only a tracked GitHub issue/epic (#19644), not merged** [zephyrproject-rtos/zephyr GitHub], so choosing Hercules means carrying a second, unrelated firmware toolchain purely for the watchdog. (2) The watchdog's actual job (liveness challenge-response, CAN/health telemetry watch, force-STANDBY, log+alarm) doesn't need lockstep-grade internal fault detection at all — that's a requirement for a device that is *itself* executing the safety function, which isn't what REQ-103 is asking the watchdog to do. Reaching for a certified lockstep part here risks the exact over-spec the task flags as a question worth asking.

**Recommendation: NXP S32K3's non-lockstep single-core family member** (same silicon family, pin-compatible upgrade path to the lockstep S32K344 variant already priced above, if the owner later wants the higher bar) as the primary candidate, on toolchain-fit grounds — with Hercules and AURIX recorded as valid, more heavily-precedented alternatives if the owner weighs safety-market pedigree/certification trail above toolchain unification. This is an owner call, not something to default silently, consistent with how D-ENT-2 was handled for PolarFire itself.

### 1.4 A "who watches the watchmen" note

Even an "independent" MCU watchdog is a single physical device — the classic, cheap layered-defense answer (used broadly in industrial watchdog design) is to pair the intelligent watchdog MCU with a **simple external supervisor IC watching it in turn** (TPS3813-class, $1.51@100 — see table above). This doesn't chase 2oo3-grade coverage of the watchdog itself (flagged as a residual gap in §6 below, not proposed here), but for ~$1.50 it catches the case where the watchdog MCU's own firmware hangs.

## 2. Rung 2 (MC-Max SKU): the fail-functional voting pair

### 2.1 Topology: REQ-104's own wording has already picked the shape

The task asks to weigh 2oo2 duplex + arbiter against true 2oo3 TMR. Worth being precise about *why* a bare 2oo2 duplex needs a third element at all: a plain 2oo2 pair can **detect** a mismatch (that's what railway "vital" 2oo2 computers do — on disagreement they fail to a de-energized/safe state) but has no way to **decide which member is correct**, because two votes can't produce a majority. That's exactly why the industrial-safety world adds the "D" — 1oo2D's self-diagnostics on each channel give "an independent shutdown path, enabling degraded mode operation" [industrialmonitordirect.com 1oo2D explainer] — or, in CEC's case, why REQ-104 already names **"arbitration involving the independent watchdog"** rather than a symmetric third compute peer. That phrase is functionally a 1oo2D shape: two full compute members (A/B) plus one lighter-weight referee (the Rung-1 watchdog, reused) that has enough side-channel evidence — the liveness/health telemetry it already collects for Rung 1 — to break a tie.

True 2oo3 TMR (three symmetric, equal-capability compute peers, majority-vote every output) is the right tool when a system must keep running correctly through a fault with **no maintenance window** — spacecraft, nuclear I&C. CEC's Hub is a serviceable $1.5–3k-class appliance (survey 7/D-ENT-3 comparables) with a real maintenance window; a third full PolarFire SoC roughly triples the compute-plane BOM (see §4) for coverage the value proposition doesn't need. **Recommend 2oo2 + arbiter, which is what REQ-104 already specifies — this survey validates rather than overturns it.**

| System / precedent | Redundancy shape | What's actually voted | Coverage class | Applicability to CEC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Avionics command/monitor (Boeing 777 triplex-dissimilar, Airbus 2 ELAC+3 SEC) | Dissimilar hardware **and** software per lane, cross-channel monitoring | Flight-surface commands, every cycle | Defends common-mode *design* faults directly | Architecture pattern only — dissimilar hardware+software per lane is a different, much more expensive product decision (see §6); not proposed here |
| Railway vital 2oo2 (interlocking, self-checking pair) | Two identical channels, agree-to-act | Track/signal commands | Fail-**safe** (de-energize on mismatch), not fail-operational | Confirms the "duplex alone only detects" point above — CEC needs the arbiter precisely because plain 2oo2 stops at detection |
| Industrial SIS 1oo2D (HIMA F8650-class) | Two CPUs + per-channel diagnostics as the 3rd input | Safety-function output | Fail-operational through 1 fault, **then** degrades to 1oo1 | **Closest architectural match to REQ-104.** HIMA's own numbers: <50 ms redundancy switchover, <10 ms master/slave data-sync cycle [beixundcssupply.com HIMA F8650E] — useful as an *ambitious-end anchor*, not a promise, given CEC's compute (Zephyr/RTOS on general-purpose cores) isn't purpose-built safety silicon the way HIMA's is |
| Dual-BMC / ATCA dual shelf-manager | Active/standby pair, heartbeat + scratchpad/doorbell sync over a dedicated link | Which controller is "active"; management-plane state | Fail-operational through 1 fault | **Closest *market* analogy** — same problem domain (out-of-band monitoring/management appliance, not a control loop). ATCA's own dual-redundant IPMB pattern and BMC failover-negotiation patents [patents.justia.com/patent/11836100; embedded.com ATCA IPMI] are a better template for CEC's *actual* traffic (health telemetry, logs, northbound reporting) than avionics/rail vocabulary is. |

### 2.2 State synchronization: checkpointing, not lockstep — say so explicitly

Every lockstep example above (Hercules' dual R4F/R5F cores, S32K3/AURIX lockstep cores) is **two cores on one die, sharing one clock edge, one power domain, one silicon lot** — that's what makes cycle-accurate lockstep possible: any divergence in a single clock cycle is by definition a fault, because both cores are fed the literal same inputs at the literal same instant. **Two separate PolarFire SoCs on two dies cannot do this** — different clock domains, independent power sequencing, and unavoidable propagation skew between boards/packages make cycle-accurate comparison meaningless. The register text should not use "lockstep" for the SoC pair; reserve that word for the Rung-1 watchdog class if a lockstep-grade part is chosen there. The honest mechanism for the SoC pair is **checkpointed state sync + heartbeat**, the same pattern dual-BMC and DCS-style "bumpless transfer" systems already use [ControlSoft bumpless-transfer explainer; redundant-controller bumpless-failover patents].

**Proposed mechanism, built from resources survey 1 and survey 5 already confirmed are free on-die:**
- **High-rate state mirroring:** each PolarFire SoC carries **two PCIe Gen2 EP/RP-configurable controllers** at every density [survey 1, Microchip DS60001656A Table 2-1]. A direct SoC-to-SoC PCIe link using the well-precedented **non-transparent bridging (NTB)** pattern — the same technique redundant storage controllers use for "PCIe-based write-mirroring... scratchpad registers, doorbell registers, and heartbeat messages" between two independent hosts that stay fully isolated otherwise [conclusive.tech NTB glossary; AEWIN NTB redundancy article] — is the natural fit: the silicon is already present, no added chip. **Caveat, marked [unverified]:** whether the MSS's PCIe controller IP specifically implements non-transparent-endpoint mode, versus needing a simpler custom point-to-point protocol over the same SERDES lanes, needs confirmation against the MSS Technical Reference Manual's PCIe chapter before this is load-bearing — flag as a firmware-team check, with a raw point-to-point serial link over the same SERDES pair as the fallback if formal NTB isn't supported.
- **Low-rate arbitration/heartbeat:** survey 5 already flagged each SoC's **second on-die CAN controller (CAN1)** as available for "Hub-internal... board-to-board/mezzanine link" use, since CAN0 is committed to the module-facing bus. Recommend wiring CAN1 on SoC-A, CAN1 on SoC-B, and the Rung-1 watchdog onto **one internal, Hub-only 3-node CAN segment** (needs its own small transceivers — e.g. 2× more TJA1051T/3, ~$0.80–2 total, the same part already locked platform-wide) for heartbeat and arbitration signaling — physically and electrically separate from the module bus, so a link fault there can't be confused with a module-CAN fault, and diverse from the higher-bandwidth PCIe link (a single link-layer bug can't blind both channels at once).
- **No shared boot/working memory between pair members.** A design rule worth stating explicitly: if the pair shared one external QSPI NOR flash or one DDR channel, that would silently reintroduce a single point of failure the whole exercise is trying to remove. Each SoC needs **its own** external flash (and DDR, if used — survey 1 flagged DDR may be skippable via the L2 LIM mode) — this is why the cost adder in §4 assumes full companion-parts duplication.

### 2.3 Voted-output boundary — a proposal

REQ-104 says "voted outputs" without defining which outputs. Working from what the Hub actually produces (CLAUDE.md's boards/spec description: CAN module-bus control+telemetry, RS-485 stream reception, northbound Ethernet/Redfish/OpenMetrics/syslog, tamper/event-log writes, Appendix-D signed-plan actuation triggers):

| Hub output | Proposed treatment | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Tamper/security event-log writes (REQ-070/071) | **Voted** — both members compute the entry, the watchdog-arbiter compares before commit | Highest-consequence, low-rate write; REQ-070 requires rollback-resistance, and a diverging log entry is exactly the failure mode voting exists to catch |
| Appendix-D signed-plan actuation triggers (REQ-007) | **Voted** — same reasoning, security-relevant and low-rate | A single glitched value here is the worst possible failure to let through unchecked |
| Northbound Ethernet reporting (Redfish/OpenMetrics/syslog-TLS) | **Active/standby, continuously monitored — not per-message voted** | Too high-rate/high-volume to vote every packet on general-purpose cores without real engineering cost; both members are fed the same telemetry (cheap fan-out) and kept state-synced via §2.2's link so the standby member can assume the active role without re-deriving history. Session behavior: see §2.4. |
| CAN module-bus control + telemetry relay | **Active/standby with continuous both-side bus presence** | Both members' CAN0 controllers stay bus-active listeners at all times (no re-enumeration needed on failover) — the arbiter decides which one *drives* TX, not which one *hears*. This is what makes "session continuity" honest for the CAN path specifically. |
| RS-485 streaming reception | **Out of scope for this ladder** | Each Hub port has one RS-485 receiver (OQ-5); giving each SoC its own receiver-per-port is a *Hub-port hardware* redundancy question, not a compute-plane one — and REQ-104's own text scopes fail-functional to "the Hub compute plane only." Flag for a future survey if ever revisited. |
| Module sensing chain itself (RJ-45/CAN/DETECT) | **Never voted, explicitly out of scope** | Locked single-path by platform design (REQ-053/§13.5); fail-functional Hub compute cannot retroactively add redundancy to a single-path sensor net, and documentation must not imply otherwise |

### 2.4 Bumpless takeover semantics — pick a stance, don't leave it open

The task explicitly asks: session continuity or reconnect-tolerated? Answer differently per output class, and say so in the requirement text:

- **CAN acquisition/relay: session-continuous.** Both members already listen continuously (§2.3), so a failover doesn't need the module-facing bus to notice anything — no re-enumeration, no dropped frame count. This is the genuinely "bumpless" part of "bumpless takeover."
- **Northbound Ethernet/Redfish sessions: reconnect-tolerated, not session-continuous.** A live TCP/TLS session cannot survive a MAC/IP failover without additional client-mobility machinery (session migration, anycast, etc.) that a value-tier appliance shouldn't build. Recommend a shared virtual IP + fast reconnect on the client side — a brief, client-observable gap — which is honest and consistent with the bounded-alarm-time framing REQ-050 already uses elsewhere in this register ("alarm within a bounded, debounced time even though service continues"). Redfish's own `Redundancy` resource (`Mode`, `Status`, `RedundancySet`) [DSP2046 Redfish Resource and Schema Guide, already the target schema per survey 5] is the natural place to expose which member is currently active.

### 2.5 Self-test

Mirror survey 4's already-built pattern rather than inventing a new one: a commanded-loss drill where the watchdog is told to simulate SoC-A's liveness signal disappearing, confirm SoC-B assumes the active role within a defined bound, confirm the tamper log records the drill as a drill (not a real event), and confirm clean reversion — the same `EN`-style commanded, reversible, non-disruptive self-test discipline REQ-051 already requires elsewhere, now applied one layer up at the compute plane.

## 3. PolarFire-specific findings (item c)

**Fabric-as-arbiter: technically possible, but state the independence trade honestly, as asked.** FPGA literature has a mature TMR/voter-in-fabric tradition [ACM voter-insertion papers; IEEE self-checking TMR voter], and PolarFire's fabric is **flash-based and configuration-SEU-immune** — "Flash technology is immune to SEU, eliminating the threat of radiation-induced upsets in the configuration memory," unlike SRAM FPGAs which need scrubbing/ECC to survive the same threat [Microchip SEU-immunity docs; EDN "Does your spacecraft flash" RT PolarFire piece]. Microchip has even shipped an application note demonstrating dual-core lockstep using a **soft Mi-V processor inside RT PolarFire fabric**, specifically to correct radiation-induced transient upsets [AN4228, RT PolarFire Lockstep Processor Application Note]. But none of that rescues "independent silicon" if the arbiter logic is placed inside SoC-A's or SoC-B's *own* fabric: it would still share that SoC's die, power rails, and clock tree — exactly the common-mode surface REQ-103's "own clock and supervised power" language exists to rule out. Flash-fabric SEU-immunity defends against radiation flipping the *bitstream*, not against a power brownout, thermal event, silicon defect, or firmware bug on that same die. A fabric-hosted arbiter would only be genuinely independent if it lived in a **third** PolarFire device — which is a 3-chip solution edging back toward 2oo3 economics (§4), the option §2.1 already argued against. Net: reuse the Rung-1 watchdog silicon as the arbiter (which is what REQ-104 already specifies) rather than building one in either SoC's own fabric.

**Free resources the pair already has, per SoC** (all confirmed in survey 1/5, not new BOM lines): 2× hardened GbE MAC (GEM0/GEM1), 2× hardened CAN controller (CAN0/CAN1), 2× PCIe Gen2 EP/RP-configurable controller, 4 SERDES lanes (FCVG484 package). Across a pair that's 4 GEMs / 4 CANs / 4 PCIe controllers total — comfortably enough to dedicate one CAN and one PCIe controller per SoC to inter-SoC arbitration/sync (§2.2) without touching the instances already committed to the module bus (CAN0) or northbound uplinks (GEM0/1, survey 5).

**Dual-SoC board cost/complexity class.** Survey 1 already flagged a *single* PolarFire SoC as pushing the platform from its existing simple 4-layer boards to "a 6+ layer, controlled-impedance stackup... plus 0.5–0.8 mm-pitch BGA assembly." A pair doubles the BGA count and the per-chip decoupling/power-rail engineering, and now *also* needs to route the inter-SoC PCIe link and the private CAN segment on the same board. I won't invent a specific layer count, but qualitatively this is a genuine further step up from the single-SoC board class, not a linear "just two of the same thing" — flag as a real, currently-unpriced PCB-fabrication-class cost alongside the parts BOM in §4, consistent with how survey 1 already treats that category.

## 5. Honesty and limits (item e)

**Common-mode firmware bugs are real and not solved by this architecture.** Both direct evidence and general fault-tolerance literature agree: "identical software may be duplicated on multiple redundant computers running at the same time, [so] a single software error can affect all redundant computers in the same way simultaneously" [NASA common-cause-failure technical reports; N-version-programming reliability literature]. Running the same firmware image on SoC-A and SoC-B means a bug triggered by a *particular* CAN frame, northbound request, or timestamp edge case hits both simultaneously — voting between two identical, identically-fed programs cannot catch a fault both of them compute identically. True mitigation (dissimilar hardware **and** dissimilar software per lane, à la Airbus's 2 ELAC + 3 SEC) is a different, materially more expensive product decision than this ladder — not proposed here. A cheap, real, partial mitigation that fits the value-tier context: **firmware rollout diversity** — never flash both pair members with a brand-new build simultaneously; run N and N-1 one-each during a rollout window (the canary pattern BMC/DCS fleets already use), so a bad release takes down at most one member and gives the arbiter a real chance to catch the disagreement. State this plainly as a mitigation, not a fix, in whatever register text adopts it — and note it interacts with the still-open signing-key-custody item (D-ENT-5/REQ-011): a compromised key pushing a malicious build to both members at once is explicitly **not** covered by any of this.

**Power faults are survey 4's territory, not re-derived here.** The watchdog's own rail (§1.1) should be treated as one more source in survey 4's monitored/protected architecture, not a new independent power story.

**What this ladder does not cover, stated plainly:**
- A shared-chassis physical event (fire, crush, theft of the whole unit) — no compute redundancy defends against losing the enclosure.
- The module sensing chain — single-path by locked platform design, unaffected by anything in this survey (REQ-053/§13.5 stand).
- RS-485 stream reception — a Hub-port hardware question, out of the compute-plane scope REQ-104 itself sets.
- A genuinely simultaneous *second* compute fault while the first hasn't been repaired — MC-Max, like 1oo2D generally, covers exactly one fault at a time (REQ-104's own "single-compute fault" wording); any N-redundant design has a residual N+1 gap, and this is where the honesty framing should stop, not oversell toward "always available."
- The watchdog itself as a single point of failure — partially addressed by the cheap TPS3813-class backstop in §1.4, but true 2oo3-grade coverage *of the watchdog* is not proposed; flag as a possible future push only if the owner wants to go further.
- A compromised signing key (see above) — process/custody gap, not a hardware one.

## 6. Draft refinements to REQ-HUB-COMMON-103/104 wording

**REQ-HUB-COMMON-103** — current text is sound; suggest tightening "own clock and supervised power" to close the ambiguity this survey had to resolve by inference:

> *...separate silicon with its own oscillator and its own regulated supply rail — physically distinct from the main SoC's clock tree and local power sequencing, fed from the already-arbitrated §2.9 rail rather than a new physical source — monitoring main-SoC liveness/health via a two-tier escalation (soft reset, then a hard force to the safe STANDBY posture via the §2.9 eFuse front-end's commanded-disable lever)...*

Also recommend recording the **watchdog part-class decision as an explicit owner gate** (mirroring D-ENT-2's PolarFire precedent), rather than letting Appendix B.3's Hercules mention be silently treated as already-decided — this survey found it defensible but not uniquely so.

**REQ-HUB-COMMON-104** — recommend replacing the undefined "voted outputs" with the boundary this survey proposes:

> *...a voting pair of main SoCs executing redundantly, with tamper-log writes and Appendix-D actuation triggers voted between the pair and arbitrated by the independent watchdog, and northbound/CAN outputs served active/standby with continuous state synchronization (not per-message voting); state synchronization SHALL be checkpointed, not lockstep — true cycle-accurate lockstep across two discrete SoCs is not achievable and SHALL NOT be implied; northbound sessions are reconnect-tolerated on failover, CAN acquisition is session-continuous...*

**REQ-HUB-COMMON-105** — no substantive change needed; minor addition worth considering: the external-identifiability requirement could name the watchdog part class on the same inspection documentation REQ-HUB-AIR-101 already establishes, so "MC vs MC-Max" is verifiable the same inspection-without-powering way ENT-AIR build state already is.
